Slow internet at home has two possible causes: the connection coming into the building, or the Wi-Fi carrying it around the building. They need different fixes, so the first job is to find out which one you have.

## Step 1: test with a cable

Plug a computer straight into the router with a network cable and run a speed test. Then run the same test over Wi-Fi in the room where things feel slow.

- **Slow on the cable too?** The problem is the line or the provider. Restart the router, test again at a quiet time of day, and if it stays well below your plan, contact the provider with your results.
- **Fast on the cable, slow on Wi-Fi?** The problem is inside your home, and the rest of this guide applies.

Not sure what the numbers mean? See [internet speed explained](../internet-speed-explained/).

## Step 2: move the router

Position makes more difference than any setting. Wi-Fi is radio, and it weakens with distance and with everything it passes through.

- Put the router near the middle of the home, not at the edge where the line comes in.
- Raise it. A shelf beats the floor.
- Keep it in the open. Cupboards, televisions, fish tanks, mirrors and thick walls all block signal.
- Keep it away from microwave ovens and cordless phone bases.

## Step 3: use the right band

Routers broadcast on more than one band.

| Band | Speed | Reach | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2.4 GHz | Slowest | Furthest, best through walls | Smart home devices, distant rooms |
| 5 GHz | Fast | Medium | Most phones, laptops and TVs |
| 6 GHz | Fastest | Shortest | Newer devices in the same room |

If your router shows separate network names for each band, connect phones, laptops and streaming devices to 5 GHz and leave 2.4 GHz for gadgets that need range. Many routers use one name and choose the band for you.

## Step 4: restart and update

Restarting clears faults and makes the router pick a fresh channel. Then open the router's app or settings page and install any firmware update. Updates fix performance problems as well as security holes.

## Step 5: look for the device eating the connection

One device uploading a photo library or downloading a game can slow everyone else. The router's app usually lists connected devices and what each is using. Pause the heavy one, or schedule big downloads overnight. Remove any device you do not recognise and change the Wi-Fi password, using something from the [password generator](../../tools/password-generator/).

## Step 6: wire what does not move

A television, a games console or a desktop computer that sits near the router is better on a cable. It gets a faster, steadier connection, and it frees up airtime for the devices that have to be wireless.

## Step 7: only now, consider new equipment

- **Router more than five or six years old?** A newer one will be faster and handle more devices. Wi-Fi 6 is a sensible minimum today. Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 add the 6 GHz band.
- **Large home, or thick walls?** A **mesh system**, which is several units working as one network, covers dead zones far better than a single powerful router.
- **Avoid cheap plug-in extenders** where you can. They repeat the signal they receive, so in a weak spot they pass on a weak signal, often at half the speed.
- For the best result, link mesh units to each other by cable if your home allows it.

## Quick checklist

1. Cable test to separate line faults from Wi-Fi faults.
2. Router central, raised and in the open.
3. Fast devices on 5 GHz.
4. Restart and update the router.
5. Find and tame heavy users.
6. Wire fixed devices.
7. Upgrade to mesh only if the steps above are not enough.
