Phishing is a message that pretends to come from someone you trust in order to get you to hand over a password, a payment or access to your device. It arrives by email, text message, phone call, social media and QR code. It works on careful people too, because it is designed around how people react under pressure, not around technical weakness.

## The pattern behind almost every scam

Learn the shape and the details stop mattering. A phishing message nearly always has three parts.

1. **A trusted name.** Your bank, a delivery firm, the tax office, your manager, a relative.
2. **A reason to act now.** Your account will be closed. A payment failed. A parcel is waiting. You are owed a refund.
3. **A shortcut.** A link, an attachment, a phone number or a request for a code.

The urgency is the tell. Real organisations give you time and several ways to reach them. When a message tries to rush you, slow down.

## Check the sender and the link

**The display name proves nothing.** Anyone can set it to read as your bank. Look at the full address behind the name.

**Read web addresses from the right.** The part that matters is the name immediately before the first single slash, ending in something like .com. In an address that begins with a famous brand followed by a dot and then some other name, the other name is who you are really visiting. Scammers put the brand at the front because that is where people look.

**Hover or long-press before you follow.** On a computer, rest the pointer on a link to see where it goes. On a phone, press and hold. If the destination does not match the claimed sender, stop.

## Clues that still help

- A greeting that does not use your name, from a company that knows it.
- Requests for a password, PIN or one-time code. No genuine service asks you to read out a code it has just sent you.
- Unexpected attachments, especially invoices and delivery notes.
- Payment by gift card, cryptocurrency or bank transfer to a new account.
- An offer that is too good, or a threat that is too sudden.

Do not rely on spelling mistakes. Scammers now use AI to write clean, convincing messages in any language, and to copy a company's style closely.

## The habit that defeats nearly all of it

**Do not use the route the message gives you.** If your bank appears to write, close the message and open the bank's app, or type its address yourself. If a colleague asks for an urgent payment, ring them on a number you already have. If the message was real, the matter will be waiting there. If it was not, you have lost nothing.

Two tools make the habit automatic. A password manager will not fill your password on a look-alike site, as covered in the [strong passwords guide](../strong-passwords/). A [passkey](../passkeys-explained/) cannot be used on a fake site at all.

## Voice and video are not proof

A few seconds of recorded speech is now enough to imitate a voice. If a relative calls in distress asking for money, hang up and call them back on their usual number. Some families agree a private code word for exactly this.

## If you clicked or replied

Act quickly and in this order.

1. **Entered a password?** Change it at once on the real site, and anywhere else you used it. Turn on [two-factor authentication](../two-factor-authentication/).
2. **Entered card or bank details?** Call your bank on the number on your card. They can block payments and replace the card.
3. **Opened an attachment or installed something?** Disconnect from the internet and run a full scan with your security software.
4. **Report it.** Use the report button in your email or messaging app, and tell the organisation that was imitated. At work, tell your IT team immediately. They would much rather hear early.

There is no shame in being caught. These messages are written by professionals who do it all day.
