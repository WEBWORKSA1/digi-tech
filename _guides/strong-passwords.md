Most advice about passwords is about making them harder to guess. That matters, but it is not how most accounts are broken into. The common route is simpler: a website you used years ago is hacked, the email and password you gave it are sold, and criminals try that same pair on every popular service. If you reused the password, they are in.

So there are two rules, in this order.

## Rule one: a different password for every account

This is the rule that protects you. When each account has its own password, a breach at one site is a problem at one site.

Nobody can remember a hundred different passwords, which leads to the second half of the rule: use a password manager. It is an app that creates, stores and types passwords for you, locked behind one main password. Good ones are built into Apple, Google and Microsoft accounts, and independent ones work across every device.

A manager also protects you from fake websites. It fills a password only on the exact address it was saved for, so a convincing copy of your bank's login page gets nothing.

## Rule two: make them long and random

Password strength is arithmetic. Each extra character multiplies the number of possibilities an attacker must try.

| Password | Possible combinations | Holds up against fast guessing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 8 lowercase letters | about 200 billion | Seconds |
| 8 mixed characters | about 6 quadrillion | Days |
| 16 mixed characters | more than a nonillion | Longer than the age of the universe |

Length does more than symbols. That is why the old advice to swap letters for look-alike numbers adds little: attackers' software tries those swaps automatically.

For passwords a manager types for you, generate 20 random characters with the [password generator](../../tools/password-generator/). You will never see them again and you do not need to.

## The few you must remember

You still have to remember a handful: the one that unlocks the password manager, the one for your computer, and perhaps your main email. For these, use a passphrase: four or five unrelated words chosen at random, such as a line you would never say. It is long, which makes it strong, and made of words, which makes it typeable.

Random is the important part. A favourite song lyric or a line from a film is in the attackers' word lists.

## Add a second lock

Even a perfect password can be stolen by a fake site or by malware. Turn on [two-factor authentication](../two-factor-authentication/) for email, banking and anything that holds payment details. Where a site offers a [passkey](../passkeys-explained/), use it: there is no password to steal at all.

## What you can stop doing

- **Changing passwords on a schedule.** Current guidance from security agencies is to change a password when there is a reason to, such as a breach, not every few months. Forced changes lead to weaker passwords.
- **Inventing clever patterns.** A base word with the site name added is reuse with extra steps.
- **Answering security questions truthfully.** Your first school is not a secret. Treat the answer as another password and save it in the manager.

## A fifteen minute plan

1. Turn on the password manager you already have, or install one.
2. Give it a long passphrase and write that down somewhere safe at home.
3. Change the password on your main email to a generated one, and add two-factor authentication.
4. Let the manager save and replace other passwords as you log in over the coming weeks.
