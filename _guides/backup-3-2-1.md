Every storage device fails eventually. Phones are lost, laptops are stolen, drives wear out, accounts are locked and files are deleted by mistake. A backup turns each of those from a disaster into an inconvenience. The plan professionals have relied on for decades fits in three numbers.

## The 3-2-1 rule

- **3 copies** of anything you cannot replace: the original and two backups.
- **2 different kinds of storage**, so one type of failure cannot take out everything.
- **1 copy somewhere else**, away from your home or office, in case of fire, flood or theft.

For most people this means the files on the device, a copy on an external drive at home, and a copy in a cloud service.

## Syncing is not backing up

Services that sync, such as the photo and file libraries built into phones and computers, keep the same files on all your devices. That is convenient, and it does protect you if one device dies. But sync copies your mistakes as faithfully as your files. Delete a folder on one device, or let ransomware scramble it, and the change spreads everywhere.

A true backup keeps **earlier versions**, so you can go back to how things were last week. Many sync services do keep deleted files and old versions for a limited time, often 30 days. Check what yours does, and do not treat it as your only copy.

## Step 1: decide what matters

You do not need to back up everything. Apps and the operating system can be reinstalled. Concentrate on what cannot be recreated.

- Photos and videos
- Documents, including scans of identity papers, certificates and tax records
- Work you have created
- Your password manager's emergency kit and the backup codes for [two-factor authentication](../two-factor-authentication/)

## Step 2: local backup

Buy an external drive at least twice the size of the data you are protecting. Then use the backup tool that comes with your computer: Time Machine on a Mac, File History or Windows Backup on a PC. Both run automatically when the drive is connected and keep older versions.

Unplug the drive when the backup is done. A drive that is always connected can be hit by the same power surge or ransomware as the computer.

## Step 3: off-site backup

A cloud backup service runs quietly in the background, sending encrypted copies of your files to a data centre. The first upload can take days on a slow connection. The [download time calculator](../../tools/download-time/) will give you an estimate if you enter your upload speed. After that, only changes are sent.

If you would prefer not to use the cloud, keep a second external drive at a relative's home or at work and swap the two every month.

## Step 4: do not forget your phone

Phones hold the photos people most regret losing. Turn on the built-in cloud backup, confirm in settings that the last backup was recent, and check you have enough storage in the account. A backup that stopped months ago because the space ran out is a very common discovery, made at the worst time.

## Step 5: test it

A backup you have never restored from is a hope, not a backup. Twice a year, pick a file at random and restore it from each copy. It takes five minutes and it is the only way to know the system works.

## Protect the backups themselves

- Turn on encryption for external drives, so a stolen drive is useless to the thief.
- Give your cloud account a unique password and two-factor authentication. See the [strong passwords guide](../strong-passwords/).
- Store the recovery key or passphrase somewhere other than the devices being backed up.

## The minimum that is worth doing today

If the full plan feels like too much, do one thing now: switch on automatic cloud backup for the photos on your phone. Then add the external drive at the weekend.
